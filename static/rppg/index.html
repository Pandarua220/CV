Measure your heart rate with your camera

Every heartbeat slightly changes the amount of blood in your facial skin, which changes the colour of the skin by a tiny fraction that your eyes can't see. Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) recovers this pulse signal from an ordinary video. This demo runs the POS, PBV and DIS algorithms from my undergraduate lab's research on contactless vital-sign monitoring for neonates in the NICU, live in your browser.

Your video never leaves this device. All processing happens locally in the browser, and nothing is uploaded or stored.

Press start and allow camera access.

How it works

Face trackingA light face detector (MediaPipe BlazeFace) locates the face in every frame.
28 skin patchesThe face region is divided into a 4 × 7 grid; mean R, G, B of each patch form the raw traces. The face position gives two motion traces.
ResamplingTraces are resampled to a uniform 30 Hz, since webcam frame timing varies.
Pre-processingIn sliding windows of 64 samples: divide by the mean (DC normalisation), 0.7–3 Hz zero-phase Butterworth band-pass, 3-tap smoothing.
Pulse extractionPOS, PBV or DIS turns each patch's colour traces into one pulse signal.
Patch selectionThe 5 patches with the highest SNR at the median dominant frequency are averaged (highlighted on the video).
Signature calibrationThe POS pulse serves as a reference to measure the pulse signature of your camera, which PBV and DIS then use.
Overlap-addNormalised window outputs are overlap-added (hop of 8 samples) into a continuous pulse waveform.
Heart rateA Hann-windowed spectrum of the last 10 s with 1 BPM resolution; its peak is the heart rate.

The three algorithms

After DC normalisation, every patch gives a 3 × N matrix C of colour variations. The pulse is a weighted sum of its rows, h = w · C. The algorithms differ in what they assume to find the weights w.

POS: Plane-Orthogonal-to-Skin

Changes in light intensity scale all three colour channels together, along the direction of skin tone. POS projects the normalised RGB traces onto a plane orthogonal to that direction, which removes intensity changes and keeps the pulse:

S = [ 0 1 −1 ; −2 1 1 ] · C
h = S1 + (σ(S1) / σ(S2)) · S2

The two projections carry the pulse with different distortions, and the “alpha tuning” step weighs them by their standard deviations so the distortions cancel out. POS needs no training and no prior knowledge of the pulse colour, which is why this demo also uses it as the calibration reference.

PBV: Blood volume pulse signature

Blood volume changes alter the skin colour along one characteristic direction, the pulse signature pbv: the relative pulse strength in R, G and B. PBV looks for the weights that give a unit response along pbv and the smallest possible output energy:

minw ‖w C‖2  subject to  w · pbv = 1
h = pbv · (C CT)−1 · C

The pulse is guaranteed to pass, so minimising the energy suppresses everything with a different colour direction, such as motion and specular reflections. The price is that pbv must be accurate: if it points the wrong way, part of the pulse itself is treated as noise and cancelled.

DIS: Discriminative signatures

DIS extends PBV with extra “discriminative” channels that describe the distortions, here the head motion traces M. They are stacked under the colour traces, and the signature gets zeros in the motion channels:

X = [ C ; M ]
h = [ pbv, 0, 0 ] · (X XT)+ · X

This is the same minimum-energy solution with a unit response to the pulse signature. Because the pulse has no component in the motion channels, the solver is free to use the motion traces to cancel colour changes that are correlated with motion, in one least-squares step. When no motion is measured, DIS reduces to PBV.

Calibrating the signature for your camera

The pulse signature is set by the physics of the recording: the spectral sensitivity of each colour channel of the camera, the spectrum of the light, and how blood absorbs light. For one camera and one light source it is nearly the same for everyone, but it changes from camera to camera. The textbook value pbv = [0.33, 0.77, 0.53] was measured for one RGB camera under white light, so this demo measures the signature of your camera while it runs:

  1. POS, which needs no signature, picks the patches with the clearest pulse.
  2. The colour traces of each of these patches are fitted to the POS pulse of the other patches, C ≈ a · h. The fitted channel gains a are a direct measurement of the signature. Using other patches as the reference, and patches chosen in an earlier, non-overlapping window, keeps camera noise from biasing the result.
  3. A window only counts when the pulse explains enough of the colour variation and the head is still. Good windows are averaged over about 20 seconds, starting from the textbook value.

PBV and DIS then use the calibrated signature. Switch to “Fixed prior” in the signature panel to compare.

Why it matters

Wired sensors such as ECG electrodes and pulse oximeter probes can irritate and damage the fragile skin of premature infants and are a source of infection. Our lab used camera-based rPPG to monitor heart rate, heart rate variability and blood oxygenation of neonates in the NICU without any contact, and builds on these signals for automated infant sleep staging.

This demo is for illustration only and is not a medical device. Lighting, skin tone, camera quality and movement all affect accuracy.
